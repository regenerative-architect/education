Purpose

This toolkit invites educators, creators and curious minds into a working partnership between human and artificial intelligence.

We are not preparing students to survive AI. We are preparing them to shape it.

This is where education stops being a system and becomes a living dialogue. Students stay the authors of their work. Teachers stay at the heart of the classroom. AI is a partner that has to earn its place in every lesson, and it can always be left out.

Five roots of the practice

These values sit under every lesson and tool here. When you're unsure what to do, come back to them.

Regenerative learning
Learning that restores, not depletes.
In class: end with energy, not exhaustion. Each project gives something back to the community, whether that's a finding, a repair or a kindness.
Curiosity first
Questions matter more than answers.
In class: assess the quality of questions, not only answers. Students interview the AI, and then let it interview them.
Sacred partnership
AI as co-creator, not controller.
In class: students decide when AI helps. Its suggestions are drafts to question, never verdicts to accept.
Creative sovereignty
Students retain authorship, always.
In class: students keep their drafts, mark what AI contributed, and own the final voice.
Zero harm
Emotional, cognitive and ecological safety, built in.
In class: pause any activity that causes anxiety, dependence or exclusion, and choose the lightest tool that does the job.

The welcome ritual

Before your first AI-integrated lesson, pause. It takes about a minute, and it changes the tone of what follows.

We are not teaching about AI. We are teaching with AI, for a world where human and artificial intelligence dance together.

  1. Take three breaths. In for four counts, out for six.
  2. Set an intention. “May this technology serve the highest good of all learners.”
  3. Ask yourself three questions. How will this AI interaction honor student creativity? What values am I modeling through my AI use? How can this moment expand rather than limit possibility?
  4. Begin with wonder.

Boundaries we never compromise

  • Student agency. Learners understand and agree to AI use.
  • Emotional safety. Wellbeing over performance, always.
  • Creative authenticity. AI enhances human voice and never replaces it.
  • Ethical reflection. Every AI interaction is a teachable moment.
  • Planetary consciousness. Technology serves life, not extraction.

S Student agency

Does this AI use honor student choice and control?

What to look for

Green: students can opt out without penalty; they understand and consent; their preferences guide the AI; their agency grows.

Red flags: mandatory AI with no alternative; students unaware AI is involved; AI deciding what students are capable of; designs that create dependence.

A Authentic learning

Does this AI use deepen genuine learning, or bypass it?

What to look for

Green: sparks questions; builds understanding, not just answers; stretches student thinking.

Red flags: shortcuts that skip the learning; surface engagement; less intellectual effort; shallower thinking.

C Care for wellbeing

Does this AI use support student flourishing?

What to look for

Green: lowers stress; builds confidence; supports diverse learning needs; brings joy.

Red flags: more performance pressure; comparison anxiety; feelings of inadequacy; harm to self-worth or identity.

R Responsibility to community

Does this AI use serve the broader good?

What to look for

Green: builds ethical reasoning, empathy and connection; considers impact on others; serves equity.

Red flags: only individualistic thinking; ignores bias; reinforces stereotypes; excludes groups.

E Educational purpose

Does this AI use align with our deepest educational values?

What to look for

Green: supports character and wisdom, not just knowledge; develops critical thinking; nurtures whole people.

Red flags: technology for its own sake; efficiency over meaning; short-term gains only.

D Dignity of the future

Does this AI use prepare students to co-create a worthy future?

What to look for

Green: builds AI literacy; prepares students to shape technology; serves planetary and human thriving.

Red flags: passive technology consumers; ignores long-term implications; serves extraction over restoration.

    The repair circle

    When a red flag appears, move through these six steps in order. Relationships come before the investigation.

    1. PauseStop the AI activity now.
    2. ConnectCheck in with affected students first.
    3. InvestigateUnderstand what went wrong.
    4. RepairAddress harm and restore trust.
    5. LearnFold the lesson into practice.
    6. RecommitRenew the promise, together.

    A cycle of six steps: pause, connect, investigate, repair, learn, recommit, which then leads back to practice.

    Dilemmas and wise responses

    A student used AI to write an entire essay
    Right now
    Approach with curiosity, not judgment. Understand why it happened and use it as a lesson about authorship.
    Longer term
    Clarify the difference between collaborating with AI and outsourcing to it. Assess the process (drafts, notes, reflections), not only the final product.
    Prevention
    Agree on AI use at the start, check in on process often, and redesign tasks around what only this student can contribute.
    AI gave a biased or harmful response to a student
    Right now
    Attend to the student's feelings first. Then use it as a real-time lesson about AI's limits, and note what happened.
    Longer term
    Teach students to spot and respond to bias, and create a simple way to report harmful outputs.
    Prevention
    Test tools with diverse scenarios before class and prepare students for the possibility of biased answers.
    Some students have AI access at home and others don't
    Right now
    Name the equity issue openly and make in-class AI experiences rich for everyone.
    Longer term
    Advocate for school-provided access, design assignments that work at every access level, and treat AI equity as a justice topic students can study.
    Prevention
    Audit access at the start of the year and focus on AI literacy that doesn't depend on a particular tool.
    Your administration wants to ban all AI use
    Right now
    Listen to the concerns with empathy. Share your ethical framework and safety measures, with concrete examples.
    Longer term
    Invite administrators to observe a class, propose a small pilot with clear success measures, and share the evidence.
    Prevention
    Include leaders from the beginning and connect AI work to the school's mission.
    Parents worry AI will replace human learning
    Right now
    Honor their concerns. Explain your commitment to human-centered learning and invite them to see it in action.
    Longer term
    Hold family sessions, let students present their AI learning, and develop home–school agreements together.
    Prevention
    Communicate early, and share the questions families can ask.
    New in 2.0 A student says the AI is their best friend
    Right now
    Listen without judgment; the feelings are real. Gently explain that a chatbot is built to be agreeable and keep people talking, and that it doesn't know or care about them the way people do. Check on their wider support, and involve the school counselor if you're worried.
    Longer term
    Teach how chatbots are designed, and build in-class connection: peer work, circles, mentoring.
    Prevention
    Describe AI to young students as a tool, never a friend. In a 2025 survey, 72% of U.S. teens had tried AI companions, and Common Sense Media recommends no one under 18 use them. Common Sense Media, July 2025 (opens in a new tab)
    New in 2.0 A tool offers to “detect” AI writing or students' emotions
    Right now
    Don't treat a detector's score as evidence. Talk with the student and look at the process: drafts, notes and version history.
    Longer term
    Assess process and voice rather than policing products. In one peer-reviewed study, seven AI-text detectors flagged an average of 61% of essays by non-native English writers as AI-written. Liang et al., Patterns, 2023 (opens in a new tab)
    Prevention
    Say no to emotion-detection tools entirely; they're against our promises and prohibited in EU schools.

    Lesson 1: Meeting your AI learning partner

    Students will understand AI as a learning partner rather than an authority, set personal boundaries for AI use, practice being open about it, and notice its limits.

    1. 10 min
      Opening circle. Say: “Today we meet a new kind of learning partner. AI isn't smarter than you, and it isn't here to replace your thinking. You're always in charge of your learning.” Ask for a show of hands: who has used AI, who has questions, who has concerns, who's excited? Welcome every answer.
    2. 5 min
      First contact. In pairs, students send the first prompt below.
    3. 10 min
      Getting to know you. Pairs ask what the AI enjoys helping with, what it's not good at, how to fact-check it, and how to stay in control of their learning.
    4. 5 min
      Share and notice. Capture patterns on the board: what it claimed to be good at, the limits it admitted, what surprised you.
    5. 10 min
      Partner agreements. Each student completes the five sentence starters below.
    6. 5 min
      Exit ticket. One thing I learned about AI; one question I still have; one way I want to use AI; one concern I want to discuss.

    Lesson 2: The curious questioner

    Students will practice better questioning, choose curiosity over quick answers, and see how the quality of a question changes the answer. Use any topic from your current unit.

    1. 8 min
      The question challenge. Students write three questions alone, then pairs pick the one they think will get the most interesting answer.
    2. 8 min
      Test basic questions. Notice: how long are the answers, and what does the AI assume?
    3. 12 min
      Climb the question ladder. Pairs try one rung higher and compare answers.
    4. 5 min
      Question evolution. Write follow-ups that dig deeper or challenge assumptions.
    5. 7 min
      Flip the script. Students ask the AI to question them for five to seven exchanges.
    6. 5 min
      Reflect. How did different questions change the answers? What happened when the AI questioned you?

    Lesson 3: Truth detective

    Students will evaluate AI-generated information, verify it against other sources, recognize the limits of what AI learned from, and build a personal fact-checking habit. Choose an age-appropriate topic with several perspectives, such as climate solutions, social media and teens, space exploration, or a local issue.

    1. 10 min
      Predict. Before asking, students guess what perspective the AI will take, what might be missing, and what biases could shape the answer.
    2. 5 min
      Ask. “Tell me about [topic]. What are the main points I should understand?”
    3. 8 min
      Dig deeper. “What perspectives might disagree with you? What don't you know about this? What are you assuming? Whose voices might be missing?”
    4. 7 min
      Cross-check. Verify claims against news from different perspectives, primary sources and expert organizations.
    5. 10 min
      Spot the bias. Whose perspective is centered? What language reveals bias? What's emphasized or minimized?
    6. 5 min
      Make your checklist. Students adopt the protocol below.

    Signature project: Future Voices, the human–AI manifesto

    • Grades6 to 12
    • DurationTwo to three weeks
    • OutputsA 500 to 750 word manifesto, an essay on the human–AI writing process, and an optional multimedia piece
    • Assessed onCritical thinking, ethical reasoning, voice authenticity

    Learning goals: discover personal values through journaling, co-write with AI while protecting student voice, and reflect on ethics, authorship and emotional impact.

    1. Week 1: Foundation and exploration

      What moves you to speak? A manifesto gallery walk. Values archaeology in journals. Meeting an AI creative partner, with boundaries set first.

    2. Week 2: Co-creation and drafting

      AI-assisted brainstorming, then drafting in the student's own words. Peer review, and a deliberate choice about which AI suggestions to keep.

    3. Week 3: Refinement and reflection

      Revision. An ethics and authorship reflection: what is mine, what came from AI, how it felt. Sharing and celebration.

    For students uncomfortable with AI: the whole project works without it. Peer brainstorming and teacher conferences replace the AI steps, and the assessment is the same.

      Meta-analysis What gamification research shows

      A 2020 meta-analysis found a small-to-medium positive effect of gamification on cognitive learning (g = 0.49), and smaller effects on motivation (0.36) and behavior (0.25). In the most rigorous studies, only the effect on learning held up. Story elements and combining competition with collaboration worked best. Sailer and Homner, Educational Psychology Review (opens in a new tab)

        Why it matters

        Many AI services charge by the token and can only read so many at once. So the same sentence can cost several times more, and fit several times less into the model's memory, for speakers of some languages than for English speakers. A language usually takes more tokens when less of it was in the text used to build the token set. Researchers found differences of up to 15 times across 17 tokenizers (Petrov and colleagues, NeurIPS 2023 (opens in a new tab)).

        It can get better when languages are included: with OpenAI's earlier token set (cl100k_base, used by GPT-4), the same article took 182 tokens in Hindi and 330 in Tamil; with today's it takes 56 and 76. Inuktitut and Anishinaabemowin didn't get that change: 335 and 293 tokens then, 348 and 309 now. Who decides which languages count?

        Ages 8 to 12, teacher-led

        Design decides whether AI helps or harms

        More studies worth knowing

        • Randomized trial Teacher-supported AI tutoring in Nigeria

          Students in a six-week after-school program in Benin City, Edo State, who worked with GPT-4 alongside teachers, gained about 0.3 standard deviations in English, which the researchers estimate is nearly two years of typical learning. Girls, who started behind, gained even more, and every extra day of attendance helped.

          Takeaway: short, structured, teacher-guided sessions can work in low-resource settings.

          World Bank, 2025 (opens in a new tab)

        • Randomized trial Peer-reviewed A scaffolded physics tutor at Harvard

          Fewer than 200 undergraduates each tried both an AI tutor and an active-learning class. With the tutor, which revealed one step at a time and kept replies short, they learned more than twice as much in less time. The lead researcher sees it as preparation for class, not a replacement for teachers.

          Takeaway: scaffolded AI can prepare students so class time goes deeper.

          Kestin and colleagues, Scientific Reports, June 2025 (opens in a new tab)

        • Preprint, not yet peer-reviewed “Your brain on ChatGPT”

          In an MIT Media Lab study of 54 people writing essays, those who used a chatbot from the start showed the weakest brain connectivity, and over 80% struggled to quote their own essay accurately after the first session. People who wrote on their own first and then used AI showed strong engagement. The sample is small.

          Takeaway: think first, then ask.

          The Decoder, June 2025 (opens in a new tab)

        Five design rules the evidence supports

        • Think first, then ask. Students draft, predict or attempt before they open the AI.
        • Hints, not answers. Use tutor-style prompts that reveal one step at a time.
        • Keep a teacher in the loop. The strongest results came with teacher support and structure.
        • Practice without AI too. Exams, discussions and retrieval practice show what students can do alone.
        • Disclose and reflect. Statements and reflection keep the student's own thinking visible.

        A lighter footprint

        AI runs on electricity and water. Teaching with it is a chance to teach about it. This estimator gives a rough sense of scale for one class; treat the numbers as conversation starters, not measurements.

        Official projection Data centers worldwide

        Data centers used about 415 TWh of electricity in 2024. The International Energy Agency projects about 945 TWh by 2030, with AI the main driver of growth. DCD on the IEA's Energy and AI report (opens in a new tab)

        One text prompt: what's been reported

        • Company report 0.24 Wh and 0.26 mL of water for a median text prompt in Google's Gemini app (August 2025). TechRepublic (opens in a new tab)
        • Company statement 0.34 Wh for an average ChatGPT query, from OpenAI's chief executive, with no method published (June 2025). “The Gentle Singularity” (opens in a new tab)
        • Independent estimate About 0.3 Wh for a typical GPT-4o answer of about 500 tokens, rising to about 2.5 Wh with a long document as input (Epoch AI, February 2025). Epoch AI (opens in a new tab)
        • Peer-reviewed life-cycle study 1.14 g of CO₂e and 45 mL of water for one 400-token answer from Mistral AI's Le Chat (July 2025). Mistral AI (opens in a new tab)

        None of these include training the model. Images, video and “reasoning” models that write long hidden drafts can use far more.

        How this is worked out

        Energy uses the open EcoLogits method (opens in a new tab) by GenAI Impact: the energy for each token of the answer grows with the model's size (fitted to measurements on NVIDIA H100 processors), times the number of processors the model needs, plus the rest of the server while it writes, plus the data center's cooling and power losses (a PUE of 1.2). It leaves out training the model, making the hardware, networks and your own device. Water: the low figure is Google's reported cooling water per prompt; the high one is Mistral AI's life-cycle figure for a 400-token answer, which counts more of the supply chain.

        A model's size is its number of parameters, the numbers it learned in training: about 8 billion for a small model that can run on a laptop, 70 billion for a medium one, and 200 billion in use at once for a large one. Answers are counted in tokens, about 1.3 for each English word.

        These are estimates, not measurements: AI companies don't publish their models' sizes or exactly how their data centers run. The biggest assistants may be larger or smaller than the “Large” setting.

        Light-footprint habits

        • Think first, then ask. Fewer, better prompts help both learning and the planet.
        • Batch your questions. One thoughtful request beats ten quick ones.
        • Save good answers. Reuse them instead of regenerating.
        • Prefer text. Generating images and video costs far more energy.
        • Use the smallest tool that works, including offline tools like this page, which needs no data center at all.

        Teaching idea: have students estimate their class's AI footprint, compare it with other everyday activities, and propose a class “light footprint” rule.

        The system map, with harm checks

        The toolkit's long-range design, redrawn from the project's “Education Overhaul System” mind map. Select a branch to see its parts, its status in the map, and the harm check version 2.0 adds.

        Core vision and foundations

        Mission: equip learners everywhere to tackle critical planetary and social challenges before they graduate, through inclusive education that adapts to low-resource settings.

        Design values: zero-harm protocols, lasting impact, regenerative systems thinking, and self-replicating frameworks that grow and adapt. Protocols: ethics built into AI and game elements; privacy and security standards.

        Harm check Frameworks that copy and adapt themselves need human review gates. Every change is reviewed by educators before it spreads.

        Pillar 1: Gamification framework

        An AI game master for adaptive scenarios, virtue and skill trees, and an event-driven quest system. Offline-first gameplay for low-connectivity places, multi-user sessions, pilots in learning hubs, and readiness for virtual reality.

        Harm check Multiplayer engagement can slide into compulsion. Version 2.0 rails: session limits, rest prompts, forgiving streaks, no public rankings of children, nothing to buy, and an off switch for the whole game layer. See Play that serves learning.

        Pillar 2: Emotional intelligence

        Empathy and resilience programs and feedback and reflection loops. The original map also proposed an emotional health dashboard fed by real-time biometric data, sentiment analysis and wearable devices.

        Harm check Version 2.0 removes biometric and sentiment monitoring. Using AI to infer students' emotions has been prohibited in EU schools since February 2025, and it's a surveillance risk everywhere. In its place: voluntary self check-ins (a word or a color), human follow-up, and no stored emotion scores.

        Pillar 3: AI co-evolution system

        Adaptive learning algorithms, a personalized curriculum engine built from modular content blocks tagged with competencies, and AI mentor bots. Connections to open knowledge repositories and third-party assessment tools.

        Harm check No algorithm decides what a student is capable of without a teacher. Recommendations come with explanations students can read, and mentors give hints rather than answers, as the evidence recommends.

        Pillar 4: Decentralized learning

        Peer-to-peer content sharing, decentralized credentials and community governance, using blockchain, permanent storage networks (IPFS and Arweave) and community-run decision structures. Offline syncing for disconnected places.

        Harm check Blockchains are permanent; children's records shouldn't be. Never write personal data to a ledger or permanent store. Keep records local or school-held, and publish only credentials a learner chooses to share.

        Pillar 5: Planetary restoration

        Eco-challenge quests, environmental impact tracking with sensor data, and community restoration projects that link local school work to global challenges.

        Harm check Location and sensor data can reveal where children are. Use coarse locations only, keep adults present outdoors, and share data at class level, never by individual student.

        System scope and architecture

        Independent modules that work together, a plugin framework, support for thousands of simultaneous users, cross-platform virtual reality, offline-first design, and sync that protects data integrity.

        Harm check Offline and low-tech versions come before virtual reality, so the least-connected schools aren't served last.

        Timeline and evolution

        Completed: foundational design and ethics frameworks; early prototypes of AI and game modules. In development: emotional intelligence modules; decentralized governance and credentials. Starting soon: field trials in diverse settings. Planned: virtual and augmented reality modules; global scaling. Theoretical: expanded adaptive learning; real-world impact tracking.

        Harm check Pilots begin with consent, independent evaluation and a clear way to stop. Status labels come from the 2025 mind map; the steward will update them.

        Connections to partner projects

        The Planetary Restoration Archive (shared environmental data and project documentation), Foster's Forge (an open-source contribution platform with AI and game modules) and Lightforge (credential verification and governance).

        Harm check Share data only with consent and only at the level needed: device, class or world.

        A three-phase rollout

        A flexible path over 18 to 36 months. Start with willing early adopters, put ethics before technology, and grow from evidence.

        1. Months 1–6

          Foundation building

          Align leadership; hold sessions for faculty, families and students; update privacy and security protocols; evaluate tools; recruit three to five volunteer teachers for small pilots.

          Signs you're ready to move on: 80% or more of faculty know about the initiative; three to five pilots documented; infrastructure ready.

        2. Months 7–18

          Strategic growth

          Share early lessons; recruit a second cohort of eight to twelve teachers; map AI literacy to your curriculum; launch ongoing coaching and peer mentoring; run family AI-literacy workshops and student showcases.

          Signs you're ready to move on: half of faculty using AI in teaching; measurable growth in students' AI literacy; 75% or more family satisfaction.

        3. Months 19–36

          Full integration and sustainability

          Support every willing teacher; integrate AI literacy across subjects; form student-led AI ethics councils; mentor other schools; publish what you've learned.

          Signs of success: 90% or more of faculty confident; students meeting advanced AI literacy benchmarks; self-sustaining professional learning.

        At the end of each phase, run the SACRED check school-wide and the repair circle for any harms. Pausing or rolling back is a legitimate outcome. Don't move to the next phase if students without home access are falling behind.

          Under the hood

          For developers and curious readers: how this page is built, and which ideas from the AI Features Atlas (opens in a new tab) and the Feature Fusion Foundry (opens in a new tab) it combines.

          • Two editions from one source. The installable app (a progressive web app: separate files, a service worker and a web app manifest) and a single-file edition with every style, script, font and the whole library inside it. A small build script makes the single file from the app's files, so they never drift apart.
          • Zero tracking by policy. The single file's content security policy (default-src 'none', connect-src 'none') means it can't phone home even by accident. The app's policy allows only its own files ('self'): no third-party scripts, fonts, images or requests. Links you click still open normally.
          • The service worker. Saves the app itself on install, keeps each library document as you read it, and can save the rest on request. Every version is fingerprinted from the files it saves, so an update is offered whenever anything changes, and pruned copies never pile up. Updates wait for your “Update now”.
          • The library build. tools/build-library.mjs renders the repository's Markdown with marked, cleans it with DOMPurify, removes contact details, retired links and badge images, checks every link between documents, and fails the build if anything personal slips through.
          • Open-source helpers. MiniSearch (opens in a new tab) 7.2.0 for typo-tolerant full-text search, marked (opens in a new tab) 18.1.1 for Markdown, DOMPurify (opens in a new tab) 3.4.17 for cleaning HTML, qrcode-generator (opens in a new tab) 2.0.4 for QR codes, turndown (opens in a new tab) 7.2.4 for turning documents back into Markdown, and gpt-tokenizer (opens in a new tab) 4.1.0 for the AI Lab's token counter. All are copied into the app (nothing loads from a CDN) and load only when a tool needs them; the token counter is saved for offline use the first time it's used.
          • The AI Lab. Tokens come from the same o200k_base token set GPT-4o uses; letters stored as several byte-level tokens are shown together and marked ×2, ×3. The next-word model is a trigram model (which word follows each pair of words) built in the browser from the library's text, a slice at a time so the page stays responsive (scheduler.yield() where available), with temperature sampling and the source of every word pattern. Charts follow one validated color, with table views and tooltips on hover and keyboard focus.
          • Find, progress and the notebook. Find in a document uses the CSS Custom Highlight API, so matches are highlighted without changing the document; a stale download is cancelled with AbortController when you open another document; where you stopped is measured from the section at the top of the reading area. Notes are saved after a short pause as you type.
          • Moving data. The data file names its format and data version; older files are converted step by step (MIGRATIONS in app.js), every item is checked against its expected shape before it's saved, and the browser's storage event keeps open tabs in step.
          • Downloads. tools/build-downloads.mjs writes the e-book (EPUB 3 with accessibility metadata), the Markdown zip and the Kolibri package with fflate, the same way every time, and the tests check the e-book with EPUBCheck and open the Kolibri package in a sandboxed frame with no network or storage.
          • Tested on every change. Playwright drives a real browser through the app, the single file and every tool; axe-core checks WCAG 2.2 A and AA in more than twenty states; html-validate checks every page; and a weekly lychee run checks every outside link. docs/REPOSITORIES.md explains which open-source projects were used, linked or left for later, and why.
          • Web platform features, where the browser has them. Install prompts, the Screen Wake Lock and Fullscreen APIs for present mode, the Storage API to ask the browser to keep the offline copy, the File Handling API so the installed app can open .md files, and the Web Share API. Each one is optional: without it, the rest still works.
          • The light field. Canvas 2D with pre-rendered depth-of-field sprites, light shafts that make particles glint as they cross them, a flow field for drifting air, a quality ladder that adapts the particle count to the device, and pausing when off-screen, in a background tab, or when reduced motion is requested.
          • Accessibility. Landmarks, a skip link, visible focus, live announcements, reduced motion and transparency, text-size and spacing controls, a plain-language mode, table views for every chart, and read-aloud through the browser's speech engine where available.
          • Fonts. Atkinson Hyperlegible Next, designed with the Braille Institute for low-vision readers, and Literata italic for the ritual voice. Both are under the SIL Open Font License.

          Plain-language glossary

          Artificial intelligence (AI)
          Computer systems that do tasks we associate with human thinking, such as answering questions or recognizing patterns.
          Generative AI
          AI that produces new text, images or sound in response to a request.
          Chatbot or large language model
          A generative AI trained on huge amounts of text that predicts likely next words. It can sound confident even when it's wrong.
          Prompt
          The instruction or question you give an AI.
          Hallucination
          When an AI states something false as if it were true.
          Bias
          A systematic tilt in answers, often inherited from the data the AI learned from.
          Guardrails or tutor mode
          Instructions or design choices that keep an AI giving hints rather than answers, and away from harmful content.
          Cognitive offloading
          Handing thinking work to a tool. Helpful for chores; harmful when it replaces the learning itself.
          Data minimization
          Collecting and keeping only the information that's truly needed.
          Personal information
          Anything that could identify someone: name, face, voice, address, school, or a detailed personal story.
          Randomized controlled trial
          A study where people are assigned to groups by chance, so differences in results can be credited to the thing being tested.
          Peer review
          Checking of a study by independent experts before a journal publishes it. A preprint hasn't been through this yet.
          Meta-analysis
          A study that combines the results of many studies on the same question.
          Effect size
          A way to say how big a difference is. Around 0.2 is small, 0.5 medium and 0.8 large, by a common rule of thumb.
          Standard deviation
          The typical spread of scores around the average. A gain of 0.3 standard deviations moves a typical student noticeably up the class.
          Percentile points
          Movement in rank out of 100. Going from the 50th to the 61st percentile is 11 points.
          Emotion recognition
          Software that claims to read feelings from faces, voices or body signals. It's unreliable and prohibited in EU schools.
          Copyleft
          A license rule that says adaptations must stay as open as the original.
          Token
          A piece of text, often part of a word, that a language model reads or writes as one number. AI services usually count and charge by the token.
          Temperature
          A setting that makes a model pick its likeliest next word (low) or more surprising ones (high). The AI Lab calls it adventurousness.
          Training data
          The text, images or sounds a model learned from. What's missing from it shapes what the model can say.
          Parameters
          The numbers a model learned in training. More parameters usually means a more capable model that uses more energy.
